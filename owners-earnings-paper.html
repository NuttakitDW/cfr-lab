Vyas 2024 — Buffett's metric, rebuilt out of modern asset-pricing parts Back to index
Author Aidan Vyas
Affiliation Rice University
Sample 1963 – 2022 · 714 months

The single insight

Keep walking down the income statement. The literature stopped one subtraction too early — the last one is capital expenditures.

Twenty years of profitability research is one long subtraction: gross profits, then minus SG&A, then minus operating accruals. Each step produced a better return predictor. Vyas takes the next step — minus capex — and lands on the metric Warren Buffett described in 1986 as owner's earnings. The result predicts the cross section, subsumes a dozen popular value and profitability anomalies, and holds no matter which deflator you scale it by.

Section 1 · The construction

One subtraction at a time, each one a paper

Every line below is a published anomaly. Read top to bottom and you are reading the last two decades of the profitability literature as a single ledger.

Revenue the top line —
− Cost of goods sold = Gross profits Novy-Marx 2013
− Reported SG&A, excluding R&D = Operating profits Ball et al. 2015
− Operating accruals = Cash-based operating profits Ball et al. 2016
− Capital expenditures = OWNER'S EARNINGS this paper

Buffett's own wording, which the paper takes as its starting point:

"(a) reported earnings plus (b) depreciation, depletion, amortization, and certain other non-cash charges… less (c) the average annual amount of capitalized expenditures for plant and equipment, etc., that the business requires to fully maintain its long-term competitive position and its unit volume." Warren Buffett — 1986 Berkshire Hathaway shareholder letter, published 1987; the paper cites it as Buffett (1987)

The paper deliberately does not implement that literally. Two substitutions, both defended in the text: operating profits replace net income, following Ball et al. (2015), who show operating profit beats both gross profit and net income and predicts returns almost a decade out; and operating accruals replace depreciation and amortization, following Sloan (1996), because accruals capture non-cash items more completely than D&A alone.

Section 2 · The argument for the last subtraction

Why capex belongs on the expense side

The theory. Capital expenditures pay to sustain current product lines and therefore current revenue. The accounting boundary between capex and operating expense turns on whether the benefit outlives the fiscal year — economically, the repairs that keep the lemonade stand standing are an operating cost regardless of which line they land on. That reading is exactly Buffett's "expenditures the business requires to fully maintain its position."

The empirics. Capex growth and abnormal capex increases predict lower returns (Titman et al. 2004; Anderson and Garcia-Feijoo 2006), an effect strongest at firms with the most investment discretion — consistent with investors underreacting to empire building.

But here is the twist the paper is honest about: the level of capex does not predict returns on its own. Every capex coefficient in the paper's Fama–MacBeth regressions comes out negative, and among all-but-microcap stocks none of them is statistically significant.

So capex is not a signal. It is a correction. Its whole usefulness in this paper is what it does to the metric it is subtracted from — and the correlations make that mechanical story visible:

Pair (all deflated by market equity)PearsonSpearmanReading
Cash-based op. profits ↔ Capex 0.235 0.345 Buy high cash profitability and you are also buying a capex tilt you never asked for.
Owner's earnings ↔ Capex 0.007 0.030 The tilt is gone. Essentially orthogonal to capex.
Owner's earnings ↔ Cash-based op. profits 0.564 0.889 And the profitability exposure survives — by rank, these are nearly the same portfolio.

That is the whole mechanism in three rows: keep the cash-profitability characteristic, strip the capex characteristic that came bundled with it.

Section 3 · The method

Standard machinery, deliberately

STEP 01
Build the sample
CRSP + Compustat, June 1963 to December 2022. NYSE/AMEX/NASDAQ common stock only, financials excluded, delisting returns corrected. Accounting data lagged six months.
STEP 02
Split off microcaps
Stocks below the 20th NYSE size percentile are reported separately. They are 52.97% of the firm count but only 2.84% of market equity.
STEP 03
Regress and sort
Fama–MacBeth cross-sectional regressions with size, book-to-market and both momentum controls; plus Fama–French-style factor construction on each metric.
STEP 04
Race the factors
Spanning regressions in both directions, then alphas against four modern factor models.

Variable definitions follow Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen (2021) throughout, with retained earnings the single exception. All variables winsorised at the 1st and 99th percentiles.

Two things stand out. First, row 1: cash-based operating profits deflated by market equity does not significantly predict returns in this sample (t = 1.87) — a direct contradiction of Wang (2023), which the author flags and says he could not replicate. Second, row 5: owner's earnings does not merely beat cash-based operating profits, it drives it to insignificance.

Change the deflator and the story shifts in a way worth knowing:

  • Total assets. CbOP is strong on its own (t = 5.35), owner's earnings is stronger (t = 5.91) — but run them together and both go insignificant. Owner's earnings outperforms here; it does not subsume. The author's own words: the gain is "incremental and marginal."
  • Book equity. Owner's earnings predicts (t = 4.15) and does subsume cash-based operating profits.
  • Microcaps. Everything is significant down there, including capex on its own — which is the usual warning that microcap results are a different regime, not confirmation.

The spanning test is the sharper result. Regress the owner's earnings factor on each rival and it throws off significant positive alpha every time, from 1.42% (t = 2.64) against cash-based operating profits up to 4.02% (t = 4.68) against dividends. Run it the other way — each rival regressed on owner's earnings — and none of the eight produces significant positive alpha. They are all spanned.

The one negative alpha is informative. Operating cash flow to market equity earns −1.85% (t = −2.75) against owner's earnings. The items where the two definitions diverge — capex, R&D, D&A, interest, taxes — are apparently doing real work, not just noise.

Deflated by total assets and read as a profitability factor rather than a value factor, the picture is more even: owner's earnings and cash-based operating profits post near-identical Sharpes (1.02 vs 1.04), and cash-based operating profits still earns a small significant alpha (1.06%, t = 2.73) against owner's earnings. Neither fully spans the other on that deflator.

Section 6 · The headline strategy

Refusing to pick a deflator

Every profitability paper eventually argues about the denominator — assets, book, or price. Ball et al. (2015) showed the choice of deflator, not the earnings metric, drove the gross profitability premium. Vyas sidesteps it: rank every stock on owner's earnings scaled by market equity, by total assets, and by book equity, then simply add the three ranks.

A rank sum is a crude instrument, and that is somewhat the point — it has no free parameters to tune. It also turns out to dominate its own inputs: in a regression against all three individual measures, the composite stays significant (t = 3.60) and all three components go flat.

Composite factor: 6.21% annual return · 5.24% volatility · Sharpe 1.19 · skew +0.57

That Sharpe of 1.19 is more than double the best of the six Fama–French factors (momentum, at 0.53), on the lowest volatility of the group. The factor is mildly negatively correlated with the market (−0.14) and with size (−0.19), and most correlated with RMW (+0.43) — which is where you would expect a profitability construct to sit.

Note carefully the gap between the factor and the tradable portfolio. Sorted into NYSE deciles on the composite:

  • Bottom decile: 2.46% excess return on 21.45% volatility — Sharpe 0.11.
  • Top decile: 12.30% on 17.84% volatility — Sharpe 0.69.
  • Extreme long-short: 9.62% a year, Sharpe 0.81, six-factor alpha 5.26% (t = 3.83).

The 1.19 belongs to the diversified factor construction. The extreme decile spread — the thing an investor would actually hold — runs at 0.81. Both are real; only one is the headline.

Section 7 · Read this before you cite it

What the paper says about itself

Preprint Not peer reviewed Sole authored Code public

This is unusually candid work, and the candour is load-bearing — the caveats below are the author's own, stated in his footnotes, not objections I am adding from outside.

The descriptive statistics look wrong, and he says so

Means, standard deviations and skewness run higher than the rest of the literature. His footnote: this "suggest[s] a flaw in the methodology that compiled this data." He notes the Fama–French six-factor portfolios replicate almost flawlessly, which localises the problem to the descriptive statistics and the Fama–MacBeth estimates — but those are precisely the estimates in Section 4 above.

A failed replication sits at the centre of the argument

The finding that cash-based operating profits over market equity does not predict returns contradicts Wang (2023). The author could not reproduce Wang's result and lists three candidate reasons — his data, his Fama–MacBeth implementation, or a differing definition and four extra years of sample. Using the Ball et al. (2016) definition and Wang's specification, he does recover statistical significance, at different magnitudes.

The composite fails on microcaps

Among microcaps the composite predicts nothing, alone (t = 1.12) or controlled (t = 0.59), and owner's earnings over book equity takes over instead. A signal whose aggregation scheme flips regime by size class deserves scrutiny.

Winsorising did not tame the tails

Variables are winsorised at 1% and 99%, and the paper states outliers survive anyway, especially at the top end — with an explicit instruction to be "additionally cautious" reading the Fama–MacBeth results.

And the ordinary ones

All returns are gross of trading costs. The full-sample Sharpe spans sixty years, and the last two decades of profitability research have been mined hard enough that in-sample factor Sharpes above 1.0 should be discounted on principle.

Section 8 · Bottom line

Three things to remember

01

The subtraction that works is the one that removes a nuisance exposure

Capex on its own predicts nothing. It earns its place by driving the metric's correlation with capex from 0.345 to 0.030 while leaving the profitability exposure intact. That is the transferable idea, well beyond this metric.

02

Ranking across deflators beats arguing about deflators

Summing ranks over market equity, total assets and book equity has no tunable parameters, and the composite subsumes all three of its own inputs. Cheap robustness against the single most contested choice in the profitability literature.

03

Take the caveats as seriously as the Sharpe

A preprint whose author flags a suspected flaw in his own data pipeline and publishes the code is more trustworthy than one that does not — and simultaneously a paper whose headline numbers you should not quote without re-deriving them.

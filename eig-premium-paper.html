Journal Financial Management
Published 50(4), 905–933

Signal forecast capex growth
Mechanism investment plan friction
The single insight

Capital expenditure is a decision made long before it shows up in the accounts. Sort firms on the spend they have committed to rather than the spend they have booked, and the sign of the investment effect turns over.

The investment factor is one of the most reliable results in asset pricing, and it runs the other way: firms that invest heavily earn lower subsequent returns. This paper builds a forecast of next year's capital expenditure growth — expected investment growth, EIG — out of cheap accounting and price data, and finds the high-EIG firms beat the low-EIG firms by 17% a year. The reconciliation is the lag: a firm that has committed to a large future outlay carries a fixed near-term obligation, and a fixed obligation is leverage. Its equity is a levered claim on the same assets, so it must earn more.

Section 1 · The tension

Realized investment and planned investment point in opposite directions

This is the part worth slowing down on, because it looks like a contradiction and is not. The two facts live at different points in the investment timeline.

The textbook result

High realized investment → low returns

high CAPX / asset growth → low future returns

Why: the standard q-theory reading. Firms invest when their cost of capital is low, so a high investment rate is a symptom of a low discount rate. This is the CMA / investment factor, and it is already in every major factor model.

This paper

High expected investment growth → high returns

high forecast CAPX growth → high future returns

Why: a plan is not a spend. Between the decision and the disbursement, the firm holds a committed future outflow that behaves like debt. That embedded leverage raises the required return on equity — the opposite channel from the discount-rate story above.

From the abstract: "We propose a novel measure of investment plans, namely expected investment growth (EIG), and find stocks with high EIG outperform stocks with low EIG by 17% per annum. This premium can be generated in a neoclassical model with the investment plan friction, in which a firm's expected returns increases with its planned investment due to an embedded leverage effect."

Section 2 · The mechanism

Where the leverage comes from

Investment lags are the whole reason the measure can exist. If capital could be installed the instant it was decided on, plans and spending would be the same object and there would be nothing to forecast. Because installation takes time, there is a window in which the market can see that a firm intends to spend without seeing the spending.

T = 0
The decision
A productivity shock or a fall in the cost of capital makes a project worth doing. The stock price responds immediately; the cash does not move.
T = 0 → 1
The commitment
Orders are placed, contracts signed, capacity reserved. The outlay is now largely non-discretionary. This is the state EIG is trying to detect.
T = 1
The disbursement
Capital expenditure appears in the accounts. By the time a screen on realized CAPX sees it, the informative window has closed.
T > 1
The payoff
Capital comes online and produces. The residual claim on that production is what equity holders own — after the committed outflow is met.

The embedded-leverage argument is the same one that makes operating leverage a risk factor. A committed outflow sits ahead of the equity holder in the queue. Cash flows that arrive must first cover it; what remains is more volatile than the underlying asset and more exposed to bad states. Two firms with identical assets, one with a large committed capex programme and one without, do not have equally risky equity — and in the model that difference is exactly what the 17% is compensating.

Channel 01

Cash flow effect

High EIG is, in part, good news about future productivity — the firm is expanding because it expects to earn. That raises expected cash flows and pushes the price up on impact, which mechanically lowers subsequent returns.

Channel 02

Discount rate effect

The committed outlay levers the equity claim and raises the required return. This pushes subsequent returns up. The paper's empirical work is about the interaction of these two opposing forces.

Net

The discount rate side wins

The observed premium is positive, so in the cross section the leverage channel dominates. That the two channels pull against each other is why the effect was not obvious before, and why the sign disagrees with the realized-investment literature.

Section 3 · Building the measure

Eleven candidate predictors, shrunk to six

There is no long history of investment guidance or analyst capex forecasts, so EIG cannot be read off a survey. It has to be estimated. The authors assemble eleven variables the literature has linked to future investment, then use LASSO to pick a parsimonious subset and take the out-of-sample fitted value as the firm's expected investment growth. The dependent variable is IGt = log(CAPXt / CAPXt−1) — the growth rate of capital expenditure, not the investment rate.

IG — lagged investment growth Ret — prior 12-month return SG — sales growth EG — earnings growth PG — profitability growth CF — cash flow q — Tobin's q CFG — cash flow growth ID — new debt dummy IE — new share dummy VOL — change in return volatility
LASSO, λ = 0.30, 60/40 train–validate split → 6 of 11 survive

The dropped variables are not weak on their own — every one of the eleven predicts next-year investment growth in a univariate regression. They are dropped because they are redundant. Cash flow growth correlates 0.90 with profitability growth and 0.73 with earnings growth; feeding all three into one linear model inflates coefficient variance and destabilises the out-of-sample forecast. Tobin's q is the notable casualty: it is the classic investment predictor since Fazzari, Hubbard & Petersen, and it does not make the cut once past returns are in the model.

The selected model

Panel regression of one-year-ahead firm investment growth on the six survivors. Predictors winsorised at 5% / 95%; standard errors clustered by firm and year; annual sample 1951–2014.

Retprior 12-mo return +0.31t = 14.27
SGsales growth +0.28t = 14.15
CFcash flow +0.09t = 7.88
EGearnings growth +0.05t = 1.70
PGprofitability growth +0.04t = 2.18
IGlagged investment growth −0.21t = −29.19

Adjusted R² of 13.33%, and it barely moves across alternative λ and validation splits — 12.37% to 14.09% including 10-fold cross-validation. The forecast is not fragile with respect to the tuning parameter.

Two coefficients carry most of the signal and both are worth noting. Past returns dominate — a one standard deviation move in prior-year return is associated with a 13.73% change in next-year investment growth, roughly double the next strongest predictor. And lagged investment growth enters strongly negative: firm-level capex growth is mean-reverting, unlike the investment rate, which is famously persistent. A firm that just cut capex hard is the firm most likely to grow it next year.

Section 4 · The companion result

The same measure predicts the market with the opposite sign

The same three authors published a sibling paper in the Journal of Monetary Economics that aggregates firm-level EIG into a market-wide series, AEIG, weighted by market value. The cross-sectional and time-series findings run in opposite directions, and both are in the papers.

Cross section · Financial Management 2021
+17% / yr

high-EIG firms minus low-EIG firms

Within a given month, the firms with the largest planned expansion earn the highest returns. Interpretation: relative risk — those firms carry more embedded leverage than their peers right now.

Time series · J. Monetary Economics 2021
R²adj = 18.2%

one-year market return prediction, negative sign

When the aggregate of the same measure is high, the whole market subsequently underperforms. Out-of-sample R² of 14.4%. Interpretation: everyone plans to expand when the discount rate is low — and a low discount rate is a low expected return.

These are consistent, but only if you keep the two questions separate. The cross section asks which firm is riskier than which; the time series asks whether the price of risk is high or low right now. A signal can rank firms one way and time the market the other way, and this pair is a clean illustration. It is also a warning about naive aggregation: a long–short book built on the cross-sectional signal takes no view on the aggregate level, and the aggregate level is where the market-timing information sits.

From the JME abstract: "A bottom-up measure of aggregate investment plans, namely, aggregate expected investment growth (AEIG) can negatively predict market returns. At the one-year horizon, the adjusted in-sample R² is 18.2% and the out-of-sample R² is 14.4%."

Everything the measure needs is standard: CAPX, SALE, IB, PPEGT, NI, DP, EBITDA and a price series. Predictors are aligned on the usual Fama–French June timing, and the fitted value is taken out of sample, so the signal is implementable in the sense that it uses no information unavailable at formation.

For scale on the underlying variable: average firm-level investment growth runs about 6% a year with a median of 8%, but the cross-sectional standard deviation is 56%, with a first quartile of −27% and a third quartile of +41%. Capex growth is an extremely dispersed quantity, which is part of why a forecast of it can sort firms so sharply.

Section 6 · How much of this is verified

What I read, and what I inferred

Mixed sourcing

The headline result is from the published abstract of the Financial Management paper, which I could retrieve. The full text sits behind Wiley and I could not read it. The construction details in Section 3 come from the full text of the sibling JME paper, which is openly available and builds the same firm-level EIG — but I could not confirm that the FM paper uses an identical specification.

Verified from primary sources

Title, authors, journal, volume and pages; the abstract verbatim; the 17% figure; the entire Section 3 construction — eleven candidates, LASSO at λ = 0.30 with a 60/40 split, the six survivors, all six coefficients and t-statistics, the 13.33% adjusted R², the 1951–2014 sample, and the summary statistics in Section 5 — all read directly from the JME paper's Tables 1 and 2.

And here is what is missing, in rough order of how much it should change your confidence:

The 17% has no reported decomposition here

Whether it is raw or risk-adjusted, equal- or value-weighted, and what it looks like after the q-factor or Fama–French six-factor model — none of that is in the abstract. A 17% annual spread that shrinks to 4% after standard factor controls is a very different result from one that survives intact.

No sample period for the return tests

The 1951–2014 window above is the estimation sample for the investment forecasting model in the JME paper. The FM paper's portfolio sample may differ, and portfolio results are far more sensitive to the window than a forecasting regression is.

Size and liquidity are unaddressed

Capex growth is most volatile in small firms, which is exactly where large paper spreads tend to go to die. Without a microcap exclusion or a NYSE-breakpoint check, the 17% should be treated as an upper bound.

Turnover is unknown

EIG is rebuilt annually from accounting data but its strongest input is prior 12-month return, which moves continuously. That combination suggests a signal that reshuffles faster than an annual accounting variable would imply, and transaction costs are not visible from the abstract.

The specification is chosen on the full sample

The LASSO selection in the JME paper runs "over the full sample period." The fitted values are out-of-sample, but the choice of which six variables to include is not — a mild look-ahead in the model-selection step that is common in this literature and worth naming.

Section 7 · Where it sits

Adjacent work, and one direct challenge

Predecessor

Lamont (2000)

Used the US Department of Commerce plant-and-equipment expenditure survey and found that firms' investment plans, not their realized capex, forecast market returns. That survey was discontinued in 1994; EIG is the attempt to rebuild the same object from market and accounting data, at higher frequency and broader coverage.

Cousin, not the same thing

The q5 expected growth factor

Hou, Mo, Xue & Zhang add an expected-growth factor to the q-factor model, forecasting investment-to-assets growth from Tobin's q, operating cash flow and change in return on equity. Similar spirit, different target variable and different predictor set — do not read the two literatures' numbers as measuring the same signal.

The challenge

Chen & Liu (2023)

"Dissecting the Expected Investment Growth Premium" splits investment growth into externally and internally financed components. Their claim: the externally financed piece subsumes the EIG premium entirely, while the internally financed piece predicts nothing and turns negative beyond a year. If that holds, the premium is a financing-side effect, not a plans effect.

The Chen–Liu decomposition is the sharpest test of the paper's own story. The embedded-leverage mechanism does not obviously care where the money comes from — a committed outflow is a committed outflow. If only externally financed growth carries the return, the leverage is literal rather than embedded, and the neoclassical framing is doing less work than the paper claims.

Section 8 · Bottom line

Three things to remember

01

Forecast the characteristic instead of measuring it

This is the transferable move, and it has nothing to do with capex. Any accounting quantity that is decided long before it is reported has a window in which the plan is visible and the number is not. Fitting a cheap forecast and sorting on the fitted value is a general recipe — the same one behind expected-growth factors, expected-profitability sorts, and the trend-in-profitability work.

02

The sign is the finding

If EIG merely predicted returns you would file it next to the investment factor. That it predicts them with the opposite sign to realized investment is what forces an explanation, and the explanation — a committed outflow is leverage — is testable independently of the return result.

03

Read the full text before trusting 17%

A 17% annual spread is very large for a signal built from six ordinary accounting variables, and the abstract reports it without risk adjustment, weighting scheme, or size screen. The construction on this page is verified; the headline number is not. Section 6 lists exactly what to check.
